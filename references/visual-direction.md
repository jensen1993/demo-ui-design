# Choose a visual direction for each demo

A demo should look appropriate to its product and audience, rather than inheriting an unrelated previous demo's colors or layout. Explicit user requirements, supplied brand assets, reference images, and established project design tokens take priority. Do not infer an official brand palette or factual business attributes from a requirement list that does not specify them.

## Decide before implementing

1. Identify the actual users and their main tasks, product category, context of use, information density, target platform and any existing visual material. For a requirement document, distinguish facts in the document from illustrative sample products or data; do not let one invented sample determine the whole product's identity.
2. Privately consider at least three meaningfully different visual directions that fit those facts. Compare color, typography, density, page structure, imagery, and interaction tone—not just three similar accent colors. Choose one and briefly explain the reason in the final response. Do not interrupt a nontechnical user to choose unless their preference is genuinely necessary or they request options.
3. Use the chosen direction to define semantic color tokens (brand/primary, background, surface, primary and secondary text, border, success, warning, error), typography, spacing, shape, imagery and component treatments. Choose sufficient contrast on actual colors. A default accent is a starting decision, not a rule to make all pages the same color.
4. Give each product or feature appropriate content hierarchy. Do not default to a low-saturation green + off-white palette, a gradient hero, or a grid of round cards merely because it is familiar. Green and any other color remain valid if justified by the brief or brand. Avoid repetitive decoration, generic claims, invented achievements and imagery that suggests the wrong industry.
5. Use iOS-inspired navigation, safe-area behavior and interaction polish as the default for an unspecified new mobile App, not as a mandatory color palette or Apple branding. Apply Android or mini-program platform conventions as appropriate. Do not use platform appearance as a substitute for the product's own brand direction.

## Examples, not fixed mappings

- A dense procurement or operations product may suit a restrained high-contrast, compact layout; navy/slate, graphite/orange, or other palettes may fit depending on the brand and setting. A procurement product is not automatically agricultural or green.
- A consumer lifestyle product may need expressive imagery, editorial typography and a brighter or warmer identity; determine the palette from its actual positioning rather than generic category stereotypes.
- A professional data product may benefit from neutral surfaces and one clear accent, with enough differentiated colors for charts and status; choose density and colors for scanability.
- A youth, creative or entertainment product can use stronger visual rhythm, playful typography or vivid accents when it fits the audience, without compromising legibility.

For the chosen direction, review representative screens together for hierarchy, contrast, content density, meaningful imagery and consistent tokens. Avoid making every demo structurally identical; preserve usability and the user's requested scope. Never claim formal brand identity or use generated imagery as evidence of real products or businesses.
